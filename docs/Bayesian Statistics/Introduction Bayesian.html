Introduction to Bayesian Statistics

“Probability does not exist.” — B. de Finetti, opening line of Theory of Probability (1974). His point: probability is not a property of the world waiting to be measured, but a description of our own uncertainty — which is exactly the object Bayesian statistics manipulates.

The previous section developed frequentist parametric inference, built on one commitment: the parameter \(\theta\) is a fixed unknown constant, and all the randomness lives in the data. This section develops the main alternative — the Bayesian approach — which makes the opposite move. It treats \(\theta\) itself as an object of uncertainty, describes that uncertainty with a probability distribution, and updates it as data arrives.

The two sections are deliberate mirror images. Where the frequentist reasons “the data is random, so let me study the long-run behaviour of my estimator,” the Bayesian reasons “the data is fixed once I have seen it, so let me study my updated beliefs about \(\theta\).” Almost every frequentist idea — likelihood, estimation, intervals, testing — has a Bayesian counterpart in this section, reached from the other side.

The one idea: turn beliefs into a distribution

The entire Bayesian programme rests on a single decision the frequentist refuses to make: put a probability distribution on the unknown parameter.

For a frequentist, “the probability that \(\theta = 0.5\)” is meaningless — \(\theta\) is a fixed number, not a random one. For a Bayesian, it is the most natural thing in the world: probability is a measure of uncertainty, and we are uncertain about \(\theta\), so \(\theta\) gets a distribution. That distribution is not a claim that the coin’s bias physically fluctuates; it is a bookkeeping device for what we know.

NoteIn a nutshell: “θ is random” means “we are uncertain about θ”

When a Bayesian says \(\theta\) is random, they do not mean the coin’s bias wobbles from flip to flip — the coin has one fixed, true bias. “Random” here describes our state of knowledge: we do not know that fixed value, so we spread a distribution over the possibilities. This is the single most common beginner confusion, so it is worth re-reading: the distribution lives in our heads, not in the coin.

Once we allow this, inference becomes a single mechanical act: start with a distribution describing what you believe before seeing the data, then use the data to update it into a distribution describing what you believe after. Those two distributions have names.

  • The prior \(p(\theta)\) — your uncertainty about \(\theta\) before seeing this data.
  • The posterior \(p(\theta \mid \text{data})\) — your uncertainty about \(\theta\) after folding the data in.

The engine that turns one into the other is Bayes’ theorem, the subject of the next page. Everything else in this section — priors, credible intervals, model comparison, computation — is detail hung on this one skeleton.

Bayes’ theorem in one line

We will derive it properly next page, but it is worth seeing the shape now. Bayes’ theorem says the posterior is proportional to the prior times the likelihood:

\[ \boxed{\,\underbrace{p(\theta \mid \text{data})}_{\text{posterior}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} \;\times\; \underbrace{p(\text{data} \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\,} \]

NoteIn a nutshell: what ∝ means

The symbol \(\propto\) means “proportional to” — equal up to a constant we sort out later by making the whole thing integrate to 1. So \(p(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto p(\theta)\,p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\) says the posterior has the same shape as prior \(\times\) likelihood; the missing constant just rescales it into a proper distribution.

Notice the likelihood — the very object the frequentist section was built around — sits right in the middle. Bayesian inference does not throw away the likelihood; it multiplies it by a prior and renormalises. This is the precise sense in which the two approaches are relatives rather than rivals: they share the likelihood and disagree only about whether to combine it with a prior.

Frequentist vs Bayesian, side by side

The disagreement is not about arithmetic — both camps obey the same probability axioms — but about what is allowed to be random, and therefore about what an “answer” even looks like.

Frequentist Bayesian
Is \(\theta\) random? No — fixed but unknown Yes — described by a distribution
Role of prior beliefs None Encoded explicitly as a prior
Core object The likelihood \(L(\theta)\) The posterior \(p(\theta \mid \text{data})\)
Meaning of probability Long-run frequency Degree of belief / uncertainty
Typical output Point estimate + confidence interval A whole posterior + credible interval
Interpretation of “95%” 95% of intervals cover \(\theta\) on repetition 95% probability \(\theta\) lies in this interval

That last row is the punchline most people meet first. The frequentist 95% confidence interval makes a statement about the procedure across hypothetical repetitions; it cannot say “there is a 95% chance \(\theta\) is in here.” The Bayesian credible interval says exactly that — because for a Bayesian \(\theta\) is random, so a probability statement about it is legitimate. The price of that cleaner interpretation is the prior.

NoteThe same data, two honest answers

Given identical data and model, the two approaches often produce numerically similar intervals — especially when the data is plentiful or the prior is weak (a fact we make precise on the Priors and the large-sample bridge page). They differ most when data is scarce, when genuine prior knowledge exists, or when the question itself is about a probability of a hypothesis. Neither is “correct”; they answer subtly different questions.

Why use the Bayesian approach?

  • Direct probability statements. You can say “there is a 95% probability \(\theta\) is between 0.6 and 0.8,” or “an 88% probability the new treatment beats the old” — the statements people actually want, which frequentism cannot make.
  • Prior knowledge is used, not wasted. If previous studies, physical limits, or expert judgement constrain \(\theta\), a prior folds them in formally instead of discarding them.
  • Small samples and complex models. With little data the posterior degrades gracefully toward the prior rather than producing the unstable estimates and mis-covering intervals frequentist asymptotics can give.
  • One coherent rule for everything. Estimation, intervals, prediction, and model comparison all fall out of the same posterior — there is no separate machinery for each.
  • Natural updating. Yesterday’s posterior is today’s prior; evidence accumulates seamlessly as new data arrives.

Why someone might prefer frequentist instead

For balance: frequentist methods need no prior (and so dodge the charge of subjectivity), give objective, reproducible answers two analysts will agree on, come with clean long-run guarantees and optimality results, and are often computationally cheaper — no posterior to integrate or sample. They are also the lingua franca of most journals and regulators. The “right” choice depends on the problem; but this section is firmly Bayesian, and that choice shapes every method that follows. The previous section made the opposite choice, and the contrast is the point.

The three ingredients you will keep meeting

Every Bayesian analysis in this section, no matter how elaborate, is assembled from exactly three pieces. It is worth fixing them now.

  1. The prior \(p(\theta)\) — a distribution capturing your uncertainty before the data. Choosing it well (informative? weak? flat?) is a craft we devote a whole page to.
  2. The likelihood \(p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\) — identical to the frequentist likelihood. The probability model for the data given a parameter value. This is the bridge between the two sections.
  3. The posterior \(p(\theta \mid \text{data})\) — the output. Not a single number but an entire distribution, from which every estimate, interval, prediction and test is read off.
